Use customer testimonials to answer a real question about your service, with genuine feedback, enough context to understand it and a documented basis for the way you publish it. Keep the customer's meaning intact, check any performance claims and make clear whether visitors are seeing selected testimonials or a broader collection of reviews.

A useful quote can help someone understand the experience of working with a business. It does not prove that every customer will have the same experience, guarantee more enquiries or automatically earn stars in Google Search.

Suppose you shorten a comment about an installation. Removing the condition about when materials arrived can change what a reader understands. We will use that fictional editing exercise below, alongside the checks for choosing and displaying feedback on an Australian business website.

Reviews, testimonials and case studies serve different purposes

A review is feedback about a product or service someone has experienced. Reviews can appear on a business's own website or a third-party platform. Being hosted elsewhere does not, by itself, establish that feedback is genuine or independent.

A testimonial is usually feedback selected for use in marketing. The business chooses where and how to feature it. A case study gives a fuller account of a particular project, its circumstances, work and evidence.

The distinction helps set expectations. A small set of featured quotes is not a complete customer survey. A customer's impression is not a measured project result. A detailed result needs supporting evidence beyond an enthusiastic sentence.

The ACCC's online-review guidance applies to feedback on businesses' own websites as well as other platforms. It warns against fake or misleading reviews and undisclosed connections.

What benefits can a useful testimonial provide?

Good feedback can make an unfamiliar service easier to assess. Someone comparing providers may want to know whether the scope was explained, how changes were handled or what communication was like during the work.

A relevant account gives that person another perspective. It can add context to a service description, illustrate a working relationship and identify questions worth asking before buying. Those are practical benefits to pursue rather than a universal conversion promise.

There is no need to invent a percentage uplift to justify showing useful evidence. Equally, adding a quote cannot repair an unclear offer, a broken form or an unowned enquiry. If that is the wider problem, our guide to website visits but few enquiries helps you check the page and contact journey.

Choose the experience before polishing the words

Start with genuine feedback and the service it concerns. Keep a record of its source, when it was given and what the person actually experienced.

Specificity can help without requiring a dramatic numerical result. An account of how a team explained a revised scope may answer a buyer's concern more directly than generic praise. That does not make a short, sincere comment fake; it may simply offer less detail for this particular page.

Ask open questions when collecting feedback for your own website: what did the customer need help with, which parts of the experience mattered and what context should another person understand? Let them decide what to say. Do not supply a glowing review and treat silence as approval.

Keep that process separate from asking for a Google review. Do not use a satisfaction survey to send only happy respondents to Google. Its contribution policy prohibits selectively soliciting positive reviews and influencing their rating or content. If you are organising that process, our Google Maps visibility guide explains how neutral review requests fit alongside an accurate business profile.

Agree what you may publish

As an editorial practice, obtain and retain clear approval for the final quote, its attribution and the intended channels. Showing the person the proposed wording and context is more useful than an ambiguous “Can we use your feedback?”

Record the agreed use of their name, role, company, image or other identifying details. Permission to feature words does not automatically settle rights to a photograph, company logo or material supplied by someone else. Confirm the relevant rights and authority rather than assuming one contact can approve every asset.

For a third-party review, establish the applicable reuse basis and platform conditions. A comment being publicly readable does not establish unrestricted advertising reuse. Google's Australian terms, for example, preserve creators' intellectual-property rights and address using other people's content with permission or as allowed by law.

This is not a claim that Australian law requires the same signed release for every quotation. Privacy, copyright, platform terms and advertising accuracy are different questions. Check which rules apply to the business, industry and proposed use.

The OAIC's small-business guidance explains that Privacy Act coverage has exceptions; neither universal coverage nor universal exemption should be assumed. For covered entities, APP 6 addresses uses and disclosures beyond the original purpose, subject to its applicable exceptions.

Use only the identifying detail needed and agreed for the purpose. A full surname, suburb or portrait is not a compulsory credibility ingredient. If attribution is limited by agreement, describe that honestly rather than inventing a full identity or pairing the quote with a stock portrait that appears to show the customer.

Edit for readability without changing the experience

Preserve the original and compare the proposed version against it. Shortening a quote can remove the detail that makes it accurate.

Return to the fictional installation comment from the introduction. This is an editing exercise, not customer feedback about Ranking Rebels:

Original: “The installation took two days once the materials had arrived.”

Proposed excerpt: “The installation took two days.”

The excerpt removes the condition about materials. Beside a broad turnaround promise, it could suggest a shorter overall wait than the original describes. Keep the qualifying context and check the final presentation; approval of the words alone does not resolve a misleading impression.

The same care applies to headings. A quote about helpful explanations should not sit under “Guaranteed results”. A comment about one service should not imply endorsement of every service the business offers.

Corrections, translations, transcripts and abbreviations need review for meaning. If AI helps prepare them, it must not invent experiences, combine several customers into an apparent single speaker or turn uncertain feedback into a stronger claim. Retain the version the person approved and check it in the page's actual context.

Check the claims, connections and overall impression

A customer can sincerely report a result that needs further explanation before it becomes advertising. If a quote says enquiries doubled, ask what was counted, over which periods, in what circumstances and what evidence supports the comparison. Do not present one customer's result as a forecast for everyone else.

The ACCC's guidance on false or misleading claims expressly includes testimonials. Claims need reasonable grounds, and small-print qualifications must not contradict the overall message. Calling a statement a testimonial does not remove the advertiser's responsibility for the impression it creates.

Disclose a relevant personal or commercial connection clearly. A statement from an employee, family member or paid participant should not look like independent customer feedback. Disclosure also does not turn an invented experience into a genuine one.

Incentives: the platform matters

The ACCC says review incentives must apply regardless of whether feedback is positive or negative and be clearly disclosed. That is not permission to use incentives on every platform.

Google Maps prohibits incentives for posting reviews, including payments, discounts and free products or services. Its prohibition is not limited to buying five stars. Follow the rules of the particular collection channel; disclosure does not override a platform ban.

Selected testimonials are not an overall rating

Explain what visitors are seeing. A heading such as “Selected customer testimonials” helps identify editorial selection, but it does not cure a misleading presentation.

Do not label a hand-picked collection “all customer reviews”, manufacture an average from praise-only quotes or imply that everyone reported the same outcome. If displaying a rating, establish the genuine source, count, scope and current accuracy. Explain relevant filtering and avoid mixing unrelated services or platforms into an unexplained score.

There is no universal requirement to publish every comment as a featured testimonial. There is also no general exemption allowing genuine criticism to be suppressed while presenting the collection as a complete, independent picture. The ACCC's review report discusses misleading selection and moderation; consider the impression created by the whole presentation.

Place feedback where it answers the next question

Match the quote to the service and decision rather than placing the same praise on every page.

These are editorial options, not proven best-performing positions. There is no universal number of quotes that every service page needs. Use enough relevant information to serve the reader without making the offer or contact route harder to find.

Prefer readable text over a screenshot containing the only copy of a quote. Check mobile layout, contrast and whether visitors can reach the content using a keyboard.

A static selection may be easier to read than an automatically rotating carousel. If you use a carousel, follow W3C's guidance on user controls, keyboard operation and assistive technology. For video, plan accurate captions, appropriate transcripts and other necessary accessibility features using W3C's media guidance. Video is an option, not proof of authenticity or a guaranteed conversion improvement.

Before you reuse Google reviews

Identify how a widget obtains reviews, which conditions apply and what attribution it preserves. Check how it handles edits, removals and changes in ratings. Do not assume that paying a supplier settles every reuse or publication question.

For content obtained through Places API, Google's policies and attribution requirements include product-specific display rules. They should not be confused with rules for a manually approved customer quote, or treated as proof that every widget complies.

If the review source cannot be verified or the permitted use is unclear, resolve that before publication. An attribution label is useful context; it does not by itself establish all necessary rights.

Will testimonials earn stars in Google Search?

Displaying feedback and qualifying for a review rich result are separate matters.

Google's review-snippet rules make pages ineligible for the star-review feature when a business controls reviews about itself using LocalBusiness or Organization structured data. That includes third-party review widgets on the business's own website.

Publishing genuine testimonials is not thereby prohibited. The restriction concerns this Search feature; it is not the same as a Google Business Profile rating. Do not relabel a business review as a product review merely to try to bypass it. Valid-looking code does not guarantee a rich result.

The practical purpose of the content remains helping people assess the business. Copying Google feedback to a website should not be sold as a guaranteed way to improve Maps positions, organic rankings or AI recommendations.

Keep a publication record and check the result

For each quote, record the original source, relevant experience, proposed excerpt, claim evidence, attribution, agreed use, disclosures and responsible editor. Keep the final version and the pages where it appears so corrections or changes can be handled consistently.

A simple review can end in different decisions: proceed to final checks, revise the excerpt, seek evidence, clarify permission or leave the material unpublished. A blank permission field is unknown, not approval.

Check the finished page, not just the text document. Are qualifiers and disclosures visible beside the quote? Does attribution match the agreement? Has a screenshot exposed other personal information? Does a widget show what the heading claims?

Older feedback can remain useful if its meaning, permission and context still fit. Review it when the service, customer circumstances or rights change; do not invent a new date to make it appear recent.

Then evaluate whether the page helps suitable visitors. Observe whether people understand the offer and relevant evidence, and review genuine enquiries separately from quote clicks or video plays. A before-and-after increase does not establish that testimonials caused it; traffic, campaigns and other page changes may also explain the difference.

Start with one relevant, supportable item rather than a wall of unsupported praise. If you need help making your website's evidence and enquiry path clearer, talk to Ranking Rebels about the page and the decision it needs to help a customer make.
