Keep an old blog post when it still gives readers an accurate, useful answer. Update it when the need remains but the answer needs work. Combine posts when they would serve the same reader better as one resource. Remove content when it no longer has a worthwhile purpose and cannot sensibly be improved.

The publication date alone cannot make that decision. Neither can a spreadsheet sorted by the fewest clicks.

Suppose your service business still offers the same work, but its quote guide sends customers through an old submission process. The page needs updated instructions. Its age does not tell you whether to delete it.

That is one of the fictional cases we will use below. Before changing a page, check what readers need from it, the evidence available and what should happen to its web address.

What can reviewing old content improve?

A useful review can correct advice that no longer works, explain a changed service and stop readers following instructions for an offer that has ended. Combining genuinely overlapping guides can also reduce the effort of maintaining the same answer in several places.

Those benefits have practical mechanisms: fewer contradictions, clearer next steps and less duplicated editing. Whether they also improve search visibility or enquiries depends on the pages, the changes and the wider situation. A tidier content library is not proof of an SEO gain.

Google's SEO Starter Guide includes reviewing published content and updating or removing it when appropriate. Its guidance does not turn a particular age or traffic count into a deletion rule.

Decide on the content and the URL separately

These are related decisions, but they answer different questions. The content decision concerns the reader's answer. The URL decision concerns people and systems requesting the existing address.

There is also a legitimate temporary outcome: investigate before deciding. Missing analytics access, an unexplained indexing issue or uncertainty about how a page is used is a reason to gather evidence. It is not evidence that the page has no value.

What should you check before changing an old post?

Read it against its actual purpose

Write one sentence describing the question the page should answer. Then read it as someone trying to act on that answer.

Are the steps still possible? Does the business still provide the service described? Are screenshots, product names and sources current enough for the task? Does the next step lead somewhere appropriate?

A guide to preparing information for a quote may remain useful for years. A tutorial built around a discontinued interface may need substantial work. An account of a past event may still be valuable as a clearly dated record, even though it is no longer current news.

Historical value is an editorial judgement. Explain the context rather than making an old announcement look like a current offer.

Put search data in context

In Google Search Console, examine the page's clicks, impressions and relevant queries over a period that fits its subject. Compare like with like: similar date ranges, search types, countries and devices where those distinctions matter. Seasonal pages need seasonal context; recently published pages have shorter histories.

The Search Console query report has reporting limits. Its query table does not show every query, including anonymised queries. A missing query row therefore does not establish that nobody searched for that subject.

A page with impressions but few clicks raises different questions from a page Google has not indexed. Check visibility and technical access before deciding to rewrite or delete it. Our step-by-step diagnosis of a website not showing up on Google explains how to investigate that distinction.

Look beyond search visits

Ask whether staff send the page to prospects, customers use it after purchase, other websites reference it or another useful page depends on its explanation. Check internal links and available external-link evidence before retiring a resource.

If analytics is available, look for recorded actions relevant to that page's role. A supporting guide might help someone prepare for an appointment without being the page on which they submit an enquiry.

Keep measurement units clear. Search clicks, website sessions, enquiries and customers are different observations. An untracked action is unknown; it should not be entered as zero. Equally, a plausible supporting role should not become an invented conversion claim.

Record uncertainty

A short decision note is often more useful than an elaborate score:

This guide explains the information needed for a quote. The service still exists, but the submission instructions changed. Search performance has been checked for the recorded period; its use in sales conversations has not. Proposed action: update the instructions at the existing URL and verify the new route.

That note gives an editor something specific to confirm. “Low quality—delete” does not explain the problem or the consequences.

When should you keep an old blog post?

Keep it when it still performs a clear job and there is no material problem to fix. That can include a narrow technical answer, an accurate preparation guide or original research that remains useful in its historical context.

Keeping a page does not require rewriting it to manufacture activity. You can record that it was reviewed, fix a broken reference if necessary and leave the useful explanation intact.

Do not assume every old post must become a broad, commercial guide. A customer looking for a specific answer may be better served by a concise page than by an expanded article covering several unrelated services.

Google's people-first content guidance specifically challenges changing dates without substantial changes and mass additions or removals intended to make a site appear fresh. A review should have a reason beyond changing the timestamp.

When should you update an existing post?

Update when the reader's need remains, but the answer is incomplete, inaccurate or difficult to use. For the quote guide from the introduction, that means replacing the old submission steps and checking that the new route works.

Start with the identified defect. Replace an obsolete instruction, check a disputed claim, add the missing decision criterion or clarify a confusing example. Recheck the surrounding explanation so the correction does not create contradictions elsewhere on the page.

For an article continuing to answer the same question, improving the existing URL is usually a sensible editorial choice. It keeps an established address available to people who have saved or shared it and avoids creating another competing version merely to publish again. This is a practical recommendation, not a rule that a URL must never change.

Avoid expanding the article solely to reach a word count or adding a new year to the title while leaving the guidance untouched.

If the update is substantial, explain it honestly. Preserve the original publication history and distinguish the update date where appropriate. Google's date guidance recommends clear, consistent date information; its Article documentation distinguishes first publication from modification. A routine review that confirms accuracy should not be presented as a complete rewrite.

When should you combine two posts?

Combine them when one well-organised resource would answer substantially the same reader need better than the separate pages.

For example, a service business might have a guide to “information to include in a quote request” and another explaining “what to send before we prepare your quote”. If the service, audience and required information are the same, a combined guide may remove duplication and confusion.

Compare the actual contents before deciding. One post might contain an important exception, a useful diagram or instructions for a different service. Preserve useful material and keep separate pages when the needs are genuinely different.

Shared keywords alone do not demonstrate harmful competition between pages. Look at their purposes and available search evidence rather than labelling every overlap “keyword cannibalisation”.

Choose a destination that fits the combined answer and the website's structure, considering existing references and performance where known. Publish and check that answer before retiring its predecessors. When the destination genuinely replaces them, map each retired address to it with a suitable permanent redirect.

Google's site-move guidance allows several old pages to redirect to a consolidated replacement. It also warns against irrelevant redirects. Moving everyone to the home page does not preserve the answer they requested.

When is removing a post reasonable?

Removal can be appropriate when there is no useful continuing purpose and improving, contextualising or combining the content is not justified.

Consider a one-off promotional post whose offer has ended, which contains no useful explanation or historical record and has no equivalent replacement. Retirement may serve readers better than leaving an apparently available offer online.

Check before treating a past event or discontinued service in the same way. An archive, customer reference or original record may still justify keeping a clearly contextualised page.

In its core update guidance, Google describes deletion as a last resort when content cannot be salvaged. That advice concerns assessing and improving content after broad search changes. It is not a prohibition on retiring an expired offer, or a promise that removing pages will restore rankings.

Removal can also lose useful visits, references or information. Document what would be lost before approving it. Keeping a working copy for editorial recovery can be sensible; it does not replace any separate information-retention obligations the business may have.

Redirect, 404 or 410?

If the content has moved to a genuine replacement, use an appropriate permanent redirect, commonly 301 or 308. If the content is gone and there is no suitable replacement, Google documents 404 or 410 as appropriate responses.

A 410 is a “gone” response, not a redirect. Neither a 404 nor a 410 sends someone to a replacement page. Do not choose 410 on the assumption that it delivers a superior SEO outcome. Google's HTTP status documentation groups these responses together for Search processing.

A friendly “page no longer available” message should still return the appropriate status. Showing that message with a successful 200 response can create a soft 404. Update links you control so current navigation does not continue sending readers to retired pages.

What about noindex or the Removals tool?

These solve different problems. A crawlable page with a processed noindex instruction can remain available to visitors while being excluded from Google Search. It is not a privacy control, and blocking crawling in robots.txt can prevent Google from seeing that instruction.

The Search Console Removals tool temporarily hides results. It does not by itself permanently delete the page or replace the website changes needed for lasting removal. Use the mechanism that matches the actual requirement.

Use a decision log before editing a batch

For each page, record its purpose, the specific issue, evidence checked, evidence missing and the proposed content decision. Then record the URL action separately, including any destination and why it is equivalent.

For your own review, add a responsible person, implementation date, verification result and follow-up observation. Leave unmeasured results blank or mark them unknown. A proposed redirect is not a tested redirect; an expected improvement is not an observed result.

Check the change, then review the result

First check that the intended change actually happened: the instructions work, the updated page loads, redirects reach the right destination and retired addresses return the intended status. Review internal links, sitemap entries and canonical references affected by the change. Ask your web team to check these where needed.

Then monitor the relevant outcome using comparable data. For combined pages, consider the old pages and their destination together when examining the transition. Looking only at the retired URL's lost clicks would miss traffic arriving at its replacement. Check which URL the report attributes the data to before adding figures together, so you do not count the same activity twice.

Choose the review period around the page's search demand, seasonality, sales cycle and available data. Keep a record of other changes that could affect the comparison. A before-and-after movement alone does not prove the content edit caused it, and there is no universal deadline for an improvement in rankings or enquiries.
