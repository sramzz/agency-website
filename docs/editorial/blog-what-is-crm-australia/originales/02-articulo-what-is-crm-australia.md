# What Is a CRM? How to Organise Enquiries, Quotes and Customer Follow-Up

A customer asks for a quote through your website, calls to clarify the scope, then emails a change. One team member has the form, another remembers the call, and the latest quote is somewhere in an inbox. The customer is waiting, but nobody is certain who should act next.

A CRM can help make that work visible. It gives the team a shared record of the relationship, the potential job and the next action. Its value depends on how consistently people use it and how well its records reflect the business.

This guide explains what a CRM does, how to organise enquiries and quotes, and how to decide whether your current tools are enough.

## What is a CRM?

CRM stands for **customer relationship management**. It describes the approach a business takes to managing relationships with customers and potential customers. CRM software supports that approach by organising information, interactions and related work in a shared system. [Microsoft's CRM overview](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics-365/resources/what-is-crm) distinguishes the business strategy from the technology that supports it.

For a service business, that might mean connecting a person's enquiry, site visit, quote and follow-up. The relationship can continue after acceptance through delivery, support and repeat work. A CRM is useful when it helps someone answer practical questions:

- Who is this person, and what have we already discussed?
- Is this a new project or a follow-up on an existing one?
- Which quote is current, and what has actually been accepted?
- Who owns the next action, and when is it due?

Products differ. Some include quoting or service tools; others connect to separate systems. A feature shown in one vendor's demonstration should not be assumed to exist in every product or subscription.

## What benefits can a CRM provide?

The most useful benefits come from specific working habits.

**More reliable follow-up.** An active opportunity has an owner and a dated next action. Someone can see that a customer is waiting for a revised scope, rather than relying on the person who remembers the conversation. This requires the team to maintain tasks and agree cover for absences.

**Better continuity.** A colleague can pick up the relevant history without asking the customer to repeat everything. That depends on recording useful context and giving the right people access; a long activity log alone does not explain a decision.

**Clearer quotes and handovers.** The team can distinguish the current proposal from superseded versions, record acceptance and pass the agreed scope to delivery. The CRM needs suitable records or an integration with the quoting and job system.

**More dependable reporting.** Consistent definitions help separate enquiries, opportunities, accepted jobs and customers. Reports still depend on complete, accurate records and a clear reporting period.

**Better support for ongoing relationships.** Relevant service history and preferences can inform a check-in or response. They need a legitimate purpose and appropriate access; holding a contact record does not grant unlimited permission to market to that person.

These are mechanisms a business can test. Installing software does not establish that it has increased revenue, saved a particular number of hours or improved retention.

## Keep people, opportunities and activities distinct

A common source of confusion is using one row or status to represent several different things.

| Record or concept | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Contact | Who are we dealing with? |
| Organisation, where relevant | Which business or organisation is involved? |
| Interaction | What communication took place? |
| Opportunity | What distinct potential piece of work are we considering? |
| Quote version | What scope and price have we proposed? |
| Accepted job | What work has been accepted under our process? |
| Task | What needs to happen next, who owns it and when is it due? |

This is a business model to adapt, not a requirement for seven separate screens. A product may represent these concepts through different objects, fields or integrations. For example, [HubSpot documents associations between contacts, companies and deals](https://knowledge.hubspot.com/records/associate-records), allowing related records to remain distinct.

A contact's relationship status also answers a different question from an opportunity's sales stage. An existing customer can have one completed job and another opportunity awaiting assessment. [HubSpot's lifecycle documentation](https://knowledge.hubspot.com/records/use-lifecycle-stages) provides one product-specific example of relationship stages; its terminology is not a universal process every business must adopt.

### One person, two projects: a fictional example

Consider a fictional landscaping business serving selected Perth suburbs. The identifiers below are educational references, not customer data or results from a tested CRM.

1. **First enquiry:** interaction I01 is a request about project A. The team confirms the person as contact C01 and creates opportunity O01.
2. **A follow-up call:** I02 concerns the same person and project. After verification, it belongs with C01 and O01. It is not another opportunity.
3. **A revised quote:** Q01-v1 is replaced by Q01-v2. Both relate to O01, with v2 marked current. Two versions do not represent two sales.
4. **Acceptance:** Q01-v2 is accepted under the business's process. The team records the evidence, marks O01 won and links accepted job J01. That does not establish that delivery is complete, an invoice is paid or revenue has been recognised.
5. **Separate work later:** I03 concerns project B for C01. It creates opportunity O02, awaiting assessment. Under this example's definition, C01 became a first-time customer at the first accepted job; another project does not make them a new customer again.
6. **An uncertain request:** I04 arrives through a shared inbox, with the person and project unclear. It stays in an owned intake queue pending verification. The team does not merge it simply because an identifier looks familiar.

The four labelled enquiry interactions, I01–I04, relate to one confirmed contact, two confirmed opportunities, two versions of one quote and one accepted job. There is one distinct first-time customer and one unresolved intake item. This is not a count of every message involved in issuing or accepting the quote, and these different units do not form a conversion rate.

The distinction matters when assessing demand or workload. Counting every form, call and quote revision as a new sales opportunity would give a misleading picture.

## Build a useful follow-up process before adding automation

Start with a small workflow the team can explain. One possible sequence is:

**Received → assessment → scope or quote → decision → won or lost.**

Adapt it to the work. A service requiring a site visit may need different stages from a fixed-price booking. Define what evidence allows an opportunity to move, and keep on-hold, unassessed and unresolved cases visible. An unanswered message should not silently become a lost sale.

For each active opportunity, begin with:

- The verified contact, requested service and necessary project context.
- Assessment status and current opportunity stage.
- A responsible owner and agreed cover.
- The last meaningful update.
- A specific next action and due point.
- The current quote reference and outcome evidence, when relevant.

“Follow up” is often too vague. “Alex to call the customer on Thursday to clarify site access before revising the quote” explains the purpose and responsibility.

Keep an upcoming task separate from evidence of completed work. Creating a task does not mean a call happened or an email was sent. [Pipedrive's activity documentation](https://support.pipedrive.com/en/article/activities) illustrates how one product distinguishes planned activities, their owners and completion.

Agree the handover too. When work is accepted, who confirms the agreed scope, passes it to delivery and acknowledges receipt? Marking an opportunity won is only one part of that process.

### Make quote status explicit

A quote may be drafted, issued, revised, accepted, declined or expired, depending on the business and tool. Preserve the relationship between versions and make the current one clear.

[Microsoft's Dynamics 365 documentation](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics365/sales/sales-transactions) describes a specific quote revision and order workflow. Other products behave differently. Whatever the tool, agree what establishes acceptance and who may record it. Quote value, acceptance, delivery, invoicing and payment describe different facts; use the appropriate system and accounting advice for financial reporting.

## Is a spreadsheet enough, or do you need CRM software?

A spreadsheet can be sufficient when the team can reliably maintain ownership, history, next actions and appropriate access. There is no universal enquiry count at which every business needs to replace it.

Consider a CRM when your current setup repeatedly makes it difficult to answer those operational questions. Examples include colleagues overwriting records, follow-up disappearing across inboxes, unclear responsibility and reporting that cannot distinguish repeat work from new customers.

Use the process as the selection brief:

| Need | What to check in a trial |
|---|---|
| Shared relationship history | Can everyday users find the relevant context with appropriate access? |
| More than one project per customer | Can separate opportunities remain linked to the correct person or organisation? |
| Quote changes | Is the current scope clear, with previous versions traceable? |
| Reliable follow-up | Can owners, due points, absence cover and exceptions be managed? |
| Existing business tools | Do the required fields and changes move correctly between systems? |
| Future exit or recovery | What records, relationships, history and files can be retrieved and checked? |

Include setup, migration, training, integrations, ongoing administration, support and exit work in the cost assessment. [Business.gov.au recommends defining requirements, comparing value and preparing staff when choosing digital tools](https://business.gov.au/online-and-digital/digital-tools-for-business).

If quoting, delivery or scheduling is already managed well elsewhere, decide which system owns each part of the process. You may need a focused connection or a process change rather than a larger platform. Custom development becomes a separate decision only if a verified requirement justifies it.

## Check integrations and data quality before relying on them

An integration logo does not establish that your workflow will work. Test the fields you need, which direction they move, which system wins a conflict and who handles an error. Check record creation, updates and changes of owner using the permissions everyday staff will have.

[HubSpot's data-sync documentation](https://knowledge.hubspot.com/integrations/understand-your-data-sync-field-mappings) shows why mappings, restrictions and conflict rules matter in a specific product. Availability also depends on the connected tools and plan.

Identity needs similar care. A person may use several addresses, and several people may share an inbox or phone. Automatic matching can be helpful, but it does not establish that two records represent the same person or project. [HubSpot's deduplication documentation](https://knowledge.hubspot.com/records/deduplication-of-records), for example, describes different rules and exceptions for different record and creation methods.

Keep uncertain cases visible for review. A premature merge can combine unrelated history; refusing every match can leave duplicates. Agree who resolves the uncertainty and what evidence they need.

## Introduce the system through a controlled pilot

Start with synthetic records and walk through the fictional sequence above. Test a returning customer, a separate project, a quote revision, an uncertain identity and an absent owner. Write down observations and failures.

Before importing real records, establish the source, required fields, identifiers and relationships. Preserve a source-to-destination reference mapping. Trial a small, identified batch, then reconcile created, updated and rejected records and inspect individual examples. [HubSpot's import guidance](https://knowledge.hubspot.com/import-and-export/set-up-your-import-file) illustrates the importance of identifiers and associations in its import process.

Also agree how to stop and recover if a mapping is wrong. Deleting an imported test batch may not reverse overwritten fields or actions already triggered in another system.

Check export scope early. An export of record properties is not automatically a complete backup. Activities, attachments, relationships or settings may need separate handling. [HubSpot's export documentation](https://knowledge.hubspot.com/import-and-export/export-records) distinguishes record exports from other activity exports. Inspect the information your business would need to reconstruct the required history or move tools; do not assume a CSV proves recovery will work.

### Automate a stable step first

A first automation might create an internal reminder when an assessed opportunity needs a response. Define its entry conditions, owner, exclusions, stop conditions and error handling. Test what happens if a customer replies, the opportunity closes or the trigger occurs again.

Product behaviour varies: [HubSpot documents separate enrolment and re-enrolment rules](https://knowledge.hubspot.com/workflows/set-your-workflow-enrollment-triggers). Check the actual product and configuration instead of assuming a workflow runs exactly once.

AI is optional. If it drafts a reply or summarises a conversation, someone still needs responsibility for checking the relevant facts and authorising consequential actions. A reliable reminder can be more useful than a complex workflow the team cannot supervise.

## Handle customer information and marketing preferences carefully

Record only information with a clear business purpose, give people suitable access and agree how records are maintained and retained. Check which privacy obligations apply to your organisation. The [OAIC's small-business guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/small-business) explains that coverage has exceptions; neither blanket exemption nor blanket coverage is a safe assumption.

For entities covered by the Australian Privacy Principles, [APP 11 guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-11-app-11-security-of-personal-information) addresses reasonable protection of personal information and destruction or de-identification when it is no longer needed, subject to applicable retention exceptions. Buying a CRM does not settle those decisions.

A contact record is also not blanket permission for promotional campaigns. Distinguish handling the current enquiry from ongoing marketing, and record the relevant permission and opt-out status. [ACMA's guidance](https://www.acma.gov.au/avoid-sending-spam) explains consent, sender identification and unsubscribe requirements for commercial electronic messages. Consent may be express or inferred where the relevant conditions are met; it should not be assumed merely because someone appears in your database.

## How do you know whether the CRM is helping?

Review the work the system was intended to improve. Useful starting points include active opportunities without owners, missing next actions, overdue tasks, unassessed enquiries, unresolved duplicates and accepted jobs without acknowledged handovers.

Define the eligible records and observation cut-off before calculating rates. Show counts and unknowns. For a win rate, state the opportunity group and follow-up period, and make unresolved cases visible. Count distinct first-time customers separately from won opportunities.

Then inspect the reasons behind the numbers. A stage may be stale because someone forgot to update it, because the customer is waiting or because the team lacks capacity. Each calls for a different response.

Better records can support better decisions. A before-and-after change alone does not show that the CRM caused extra revenue or faster service; demand, staffing, offers and other changes may also matter.

## Start with the relationship and the next action

Choose one part of the customer journey that is difficult to manage. Agree what each record means, who owns the next action and what evidence closes the loop. Test that process before extending it.
